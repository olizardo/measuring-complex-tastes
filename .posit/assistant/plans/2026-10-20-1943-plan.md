### Body: Results → Implications → Limitations → Future Openings

**1. Aspect Separability and Four-Dimensional Structure**
- **Finding:** RV = 0.35 (pref–cons), 0.01 (eval–others); four dimensions emerge (highbrow–popular, volume, engagement divide, status valuation)
- **Substantive implication:** Cultural judgment is not unified. Taste is multidimensional in a specific way: prestige hierarchy operates across preference and consumption, breadth is separate from prestige, engagement divides by genre world, and status evaluation is its own axis
- **Theoretical implication:** Bourdieu's homology claim (social position maps onto taste positions) is partially supported—the prestige dimension clearly stratifies—but dimension 4 (status evaluation) is barely connected to liking/listening, suggesting evaluation follows its own logic independent of cultural consumption
- **Limitation:** The RV coefficient measures respondent-similarity alignment, not validation against direct ambivalence/guilt measures. We *interpret* $W_s(i)$ (aspect-disagreement scores) as complex taste, but have no external criterion
- **Future opening:** Direct survey items on ambivalence or guilt; behavioral measures of evaluation (how respondents describe genres); test whether geometric scores predict actual patterns of mixed taste behavior

**2. Widespread Disagreement but Modest Demographic Stratification**
- **Finding:** Disagreement pervades the sample (10+ genres per person in complex configurations); but demographics explain only ~10% of disagreement variation (Ω) and ~4% of coherence (Γ)
- **Substantive implication:** Complex taste is not primarily a luxury of the privileged. Race, age, education, and class show associations, but the largest effects concentrate in particular genres (rap/hip-hop and indie rock for age; diaspora genres for race). This suggests disagreement is driven partly by engagement with specific cultural worlds, not just overall social position
- **Theoretical implication:** A population with separable aspects can generate widespread individual disagreement that carries little social signature. This qualifies Bourdieu's prediction. Social structure is visible in *where* people position themselves on the highbrow–popular axis, but not as clearly in *how much* their aspects disagree
- **Limitation:** Cross-sectional design. We observe associations, not causation. Omitted variables (family taste history, peer influence, identity work) could drive both disagreement and demographic position. Genre-level race findings are ecological—they describe which genres have racially distinct audiences, not how individual respondents' race relates to their taste positions. Each genre position is computed from the same respondents we tabulate by race, so part of the correlation is built in
- **Future opening:** Panel data to track whether aspects converge or diverge as people age or change contexts; split-sample checks to isolate individual-level race/gender effects; respondent-level analysis with survey weights; analysis of taste formation (family, peer, identity) to test whether it drives the associations

**3. The Individual vs. Genre-Level Contrast (Aggregation Effect)**
- **Finding:** At the genre level, aspects align closely (RV = 0.92 for pref–cons, 0.53 for pref–eval). At the individual level, they separate (RV = 0.35 and 0.01). This apparent contradiction is an aggregation effect: a genre's likers, listeners, and status-granters overlap but individuals don't occupy all three roles
- **Substantive implication:** A genre can look coherent in aggregate—opera fans tend to like and listen to opera and grant its fans high status—while the individuals within it are split. Some like without listening, others listen without granting status. This shows why unit of analysis matters. Genre-audience composition and individual taste portfolio are different objects
- **Limitation:** The individual-level Dimension 3 (engagement divide) is racially patterned (diaspora vs. guitar-rock split). The genre-level Dimension 4 (status valuation) is also ordered by Black share of the liking audience. But we haven't verified these are the same dimension across levels. The coherence on pref–cons at the genre level might not hold at the individual level on all dimensions
- **Future opening:** Verify D3 and D4 correspondence across levels; explore whether genre-position variation can be explained by audience composition or other genre features (history, geography, demographic clustering); respondent-level analysis to show how individuals within cohesive-genre audiences still disagree

**4. The Measure Itself: Geometric vs. Categorical Complexity**
- **Finding:** Aspect-disagreement scores ($W_s$) and Ma's configurations agree modestly (~0.25–0.63 depending on dimension). Disagreement is widespread by construction—Ma counts any aspect-departure as complex
- **Substantive implication:** Two complementary measures. Ma's categorical approach answers "What kinds of taste combinations does this person hold?" The geometric measure answers "How far do this person's aspects pull apart and in which direction?" The two agree on some dimensions (genre-based disagreement correlates 0.61–0.63 with configuration counts on D1 and D3) but not others (coherence, which is ~85% of disagreement, barely correlates with counts because Ma's framework doesn't have a cross-genre term). Neither is "complete"; both are useful for different questions
- **Limitation:** No outside criterion for "complex taste." The codebook has no direct ambivalence or guilt item. Unfamiliarity is coded as non-liking, so the preference prevalence partly reflects familiarity, not taste. Evaluation is a constructed binary (a judgment call on coding). Some agreement is built in: Dimension 2 draws mainly on liking/listening answers that also define the volume count, so the correlation says more about axis construction than meaning
- **Future opening:** Alternate coding schemes (e.g., different evaluation thresholds) for sensitivity; observed or behavioral measures (e.g., open-response descriptions of genre audiences); direct ambivalence items in new surveys; validation against real consumption behavior and taste-talk

**5. Scope and Temporal Reach**
- **Finding:** One online survey, 2012, 2,276 adults, unweighted; 20 genres; 412 supplementary respondents (constant in at least one aspect); dimension stability (0.87–0.97 correlations when refitting with them as active)
- **Substantive implication:** The study documents taste structure in a snapshot of U.S. adults in a single year. We don't know whether the four dimensions persist, or whether complex configurations are stable across the life course. The results apply to the specific set of 20 genres studied
- **Limitation:** Can't order aspects in time (panel attrition). Can't show whether configurations persist, emerge, or shift. Age patterns are cross-sectional comparisons, not trajectories. Twenty genres and U.S. only limit generalization. Online survey mode (author-confirmed but unweighted) may oversample certain populations; unweighted results describe the sample, not the population
- **Future opening:** Panel or repeated surveys to track aspect convergence/divergence over time; other cultural fields (film, fashion, visual art, food) to test whether taste structure is distinctive to music or general; international comparison; weighted or in-person surveys for population estimates

**6. Statistical Fragility and Interdependence**
- **Finding:** Aggregate weighting was chosen after viewing alternatives' correlation with configuration counts (exploratory). Log transformation removes upper-class effect. Outcomes are right-skewed (skewness 0.8–2.5). Genre-level models share respondents (errors correlated). Configuration cells overlap (same person holds different configurations across genres)
- **Substantive implication:** The findings are robust to the main analytic choices, but not bulletproof. The results propose a measurement approach for testing in future samples, rather than settling a claim
- **Limitation:** The aggregate results rest partly on an exploratory weighting choice. Unweighted, log, and dimension-specific fits sometimes differ on which coefficients survive Holm correction. Normal-approximation CIs may be loose given right-skewness. Genre coefficients are not independent (same respondents). Configuration counts are descriptive, not statistical tests, because cells overlap
- **Future opening:** Robustness checks in new samples with pre-specified hypotheses; respondent-level analysis for within-cell overlaps; bootstrap or permutation tests for configuration counts; GLM or Poisson models for count data

**7. Implications for Taste Theory**
- **Omnivorousness:** Breadth of liking and breadth of status recognition are on separate axes. A model treating consumption breadth as a proxy for how someone values genres would miss half the structure
- **Bourdieu and stratification:** The highbrow–popular hierarchy is clearly stratified by age and class. But disagreement itself—how far apart the aspects pull—is only modestly linked to social position. A class-based account of taste needs to explain both the hierarchy (which is stratified) and the divergence (which is not)
- **Cultural fragmentation:** The results suggest cultural judgment is less unified than a single taste dimension would imply. Genres vary in how their audiences align (opera coherent, hip-hop split). People vary in their aspect-alignment (some have integrated taste, others split). This is one proposal for measuring fragmentation; other samples and fields can test it
- **Unit of analysis matters:** Genres and individuals are different units. Aggregate patterns don't determine individual patterns. Claims about genre audiences need not hold for the individuals in them
